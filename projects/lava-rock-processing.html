Project overview

The project addressed a production need for lava sand with a grain size of 500 µm or smaller for skincare products. The capstone explored producing that material from local lava rock, combining washing, crushing, drying, and screening in a semi-automated process.

The design had to account for variable rock strength, a corrosive operating environment, maintenance access, and a budget below $30,000. I developed the crushers and their support structure, redesigned the existing sand dryer, and assessed the complete process.

The final system

The final crushing unit places the jaw crusher directly above the hammer mill on a two-level steel frame. A feed conveyor brings washed rock to the jaw crusher; gravity and funnels guide the crushed material into the hammer mill and then into a collection container.

The collected sand is transferred manually to the geothermal dryer, then to the existing screening machine. Oversize particles return to the crusher for further reduction. This layout replaced the longer conveyor arrangements explored earlier in the project.

The hammer mill uses a 3 mm screen opening to reduce clogging from moisture remaining after washing. The separate final screen selects particles ≤500 µm, with oversize material returned for further crushing.

01 / Physical experiments

Material testing

The crushing loads started with physical tests on lava rock from the intended source. I broke one larger rock into three samples for the primary-crusher study, then selected four smaller fragments for the secondary stage.

For each sample, I recorded its mass, measured volume by water displacement, and estimated its thickness and contact area. A compression-testing machine applied load until the material fractured. The peak load and estimated contact area gave the primary samples’ compressive strength.

The samples retained their irregular shape to resemble the feed material. Their surface protrusions broke down as loading began, changing the contact. The results therefore describe these specimens and informed the design allowance used in the calculations.

The measured response

The recorded load–time curve for sample 2 shows the load building through several rises and drops before reaching its reported peak of 8.51 kN. The large drop after the peak captures a change in the specimen’s load-carrying response during crushing.

The three specimens varied substantially in estimated strength. To account for the small sample set and variable porosity, I added 25% to the measured mean and used 1.91 MPa in the jaw-crusher load calculations.

02 / Primary crusher

Jaw-crusher design

The primary crusher reduces the largest rocks before they enter the hammer mill. I selected a single-toggle mechanism for its compact arrangement and developed two alternatives: an eccentric drive at the top of the moving jaw, and a drive connected through the toggle behind the jaw.

The final design uses the upper-shaft arrangement. Its 50 × 20 cm inlet sets the maximum input cross-section, while spacers behind the toggle bracket adjust the discharge gap to approximately 2 cm or smaller.

From rock strength to mechanism loads

I divided the jaw into five estimated crushing positions. The adjusted rock strength and estimated contact areas gave an approximate design load of 46 kN per position. As material moves down through the chamber, its size and the location of the crushing load change.

I modeled the mechanism as a four-bar linkage in MATLAB, using dimensions and mass properties from SolidWorks. The eccentric-shaft connection changes the effective geometry through a revolution, so I recorded the dimensions at successive shaft angles. I first used 30° increments, then refined them to 15° for the later calculations.

The model determined joint forces and the direction of jaw motion at each crushing position. These forces became inputs to the shaft calculations and structural studies. The selected drive used a 3 hp motor, a 3:1 pulley ratio, and a design shaft speed of 200 rpm.

Shaft durability and maintenance

The lower shaft load favored the upper-shaft concept. After updating the geometry, I recalculated fatigue at the shaft shoulders with the Goodman method. The reported final factors of safety were 1.48 and 1.54, with calculated lives of approximately 8.88 and 11.18 million cycles for the two shoulders.

I also checked shaft slope against the limits for the selected deep-groove ball bearings and cylindrical roller bearings. These checks addressed alignment as well as strength.

The housing uses bolted plates so the crusher can be taken apart for inspection. The toggle assembly uses pinned plates and a bolted bracket; inserting spacers adjusts the gap. The impact plates are separately removable. The report’s design also omits a flywheel, based on the relatively low crushing strength measured for this lava rock.

03 / Structural analysis

Jaw-crusher FEA

I used finite element analysis in SolidWorks to examine the housing, moving jaw, toggle assembly, and impact plate under the calculated loads. The workflow connected the material tests and MATLAB mechanism model to specific loads on the CAD components.

Component thickness was revised through 10 mm, 12 mm, and 15 mm steel iterations. Static stress results and fatigue studies informed the selected 15 mm jaw-crusher construction.

04 / Secondary crusher

Hammer-mill design

The second crusher uses swinging hammers to reduce the jaw-crusher output further. I designed the rotor around a 600 rpm shaft and approximately 520 g hammers, with an intended feed cross-section of about 2 × 5 cm.

The concept evolved from an angled-entry chamber to a top-fed chamber with replaceable impact plates and a larger screen area. The final 3 mm screen opening addresses the risk of damp material clogging a much finer screen. Final 500 µm selection happens in the separate screening machine.

Rotor and chamber construction

The hammer-support discs are keyed to the shaft and retained by nuts. Pins, spacers, and cotter pins locate the hammers. This assembly can be dismantled without cutting welded connections.

The chamber is also bolted together. Removing the side plates releases the screen and upper impact plates from their retaining arrangement. The design specifies 10 mm steel for the mill components and 15 mm steel for the hammers, with access for replacing parts and restoring protective coatings.

Shaft and component checks

I evaluated fatigue at the mill shaft’s shoulders using the Goodman method, obtaining reported factors of safety of 12.17 and 5.57. Although these values suggested that a smaller shaft might be possible, reducing it would also reduce the keyway size and increase local stress. I retained the larger diameter.

The shaft calculation also checked slope against the 0.003 rad limit used for the selected ball bearings. For the chamber parts and a hammer, I used a separate 3 kN load case in SolidWorks.

05 / Structure and integration

The shared support frame

The frame holds the jaw crusher above the hammer mill so material can transfer by gravity. I compared UPN 50, 65, 80, and 100 channel sections through static and fatigue studies, selecting UPN 100 for the final design.

The support loading included the jaw mechanism’s calculated shaking force: approximately 46 kN at −16°. The FEA checked how that loading passed through the two support levels, welded connections, and fixed feet.

The static result concentrated attention around the channel joints. The fatigue study then examined the frame under fully reversed loading, with localized lower-life regions at the joints in a plot spanning 30–60 million cycles.

06 / Thermal design

Geothermal sand drying

The dryer redesign uses the hot-water supply already available from the nearby geothermal plant. I retained the insulated container concept and replaced the single-element arrangement with three vertical heating elements made from 316 stainless steel.

Water at approximately 80°C circulates through the hollow elements. Their vertical arrangement and detachable hose connections provide access to the elements, while the stainless steel addresses corrosion without a coating on the heat-transfer surfaces.

This part of the project builds on an existing dryer developed by another engineer. My work was the three-element redesign and the calculations to investigate heat distribution and drying.

A 400-section thermal model

I divided the container into 400 sections and assumed an initial sand moisture content of 10%. The model calculated the temperature distribution over 24 hours and the heat delivered to each section.

The temperature calculation showed that the regions farthest from the elements reached approximately 64°C after 24 hours, while the areas next to the elements were warmer. The container therefore did not reach a uniform temperature over that interval.

Temperature alone does not establish drying. I also calculated the energy needed to evaporate the assumed water content in each section and compared it with the modeled heat supply.

What the thermal study established

Under the model’s assumptions, the calculated energy supply was insufficient to evaporate the moisture throughout the container. The study therefore did not establish a complete drying time or demonstrate an efficiency improvement for the redesign.

The existing single-element dryer was operating successfully, so the report identifies further experiments as necessary to reconcile the model with the physical drying process. This result defined what could be concluded from the calculation and what needed measurement.

Prototype and outcomes

I also built a physical prototype as part of the project. Alongside the rock-sample experiments, the work brought together CAD, mechanism analysis, shaft design, structural simulation, thermal modeling, and prototyping.

The final report documents the proposed system and its cost estimate of $19,060, including a $2,000 allowance. That estimate assumes in-house welding and assembly and reuse of the existing pressure washer and sand screen.

The result is a complete engineering study of the crushing and drying process, with a compact stacked layout, accessible wear components, and calculations connecting the material tests to the mechanical design.
