Overview & role

Satellites that inspect or assemble hardware in orbit need precise, fuel-aware planar maneuvering, but that behavior is expensive to test in space. I designed and built a ground testbed that floats a cubic platform on three air bearings over an epoxy floor, giving near-frictionless translation in x–y and rotation about the vertical axis. I used the testbed to validate a mixed-integer model-predictive controller for eight binary cold-gas thrusters against a digital twin.

I designed and built the entire testbed myself — the mechanical structure, pneumatics, electronics, and the control and simulation software. Advisors provided guidance.

The work was supported in part by NASA KY EPSCoR and is documented in my M.S. thesis, Simulation-to-Hardware Validation of MPC for Binary Thruster 2D CubeSat Control.

Mechanical design

Structure, packaging, and air-bearing support

The chassis is a 290 × 290 × 150 mm frame of 2020 aluminum extrusion, assembled with corner brackets and T-nuts. Two internal decks and four side panels are 3 mm 3D-printed PLA, fastened with brass heat-set inserts. Level 1 carries the pneumatic manifold; Level 2, 50 mm above, carries the solenoids and electronics — isolating tubing from wiring and leaving a clear perimeter for the thrusters.

A triangular 6061 aluminum plate under the chassis holds three 40 mm porous-carbon air bearings. Radial grooves machined at 0°, 120°, and −120° let each bearing slide for load balance.

I built the design in Autodesk Fusion 360 and used the model for packaging, interference checks, hole locations and tolerances, and to export thruster geometry into the digital twin.

Pneumatics & thrusters

Dual-pressure supply and in-situ thrust calibration

A 40 ft³ cylinder at about 2015 psi is regulated to about 80 psi, filtered, and split at a wye. A bearing leg is regulated to about 60 psi and a thruster leg to about 40 psi, with independent shutoffs so the two systems can be tested separately. Zero-grade air protects the porous-carbon bearing surfaces from moisture and oil. I designed a load-cell fixture and characterized each thruster in place on the vehicle, so line losses and regulator behavior were included. Steady-state thrust ranged from 0.428 to 0.484 N (mean 0.451 N), with up to 13.2% spread between thrusters. Those measured values are supplied to the controller rather than trimmed out in hardware.

Electronics & integration

Onboard power, command path, and motion capture

The electronics are hand-built: a point-to-point soldered and wired prototype board serves as the power and signal hub. Two Milwaukee M12 packs separate the actuation bus (12 V to the solenoids and indicator LEDs) from the electronics bus (5 V and 3.9 V rails), so valve current spikes do not disturb the controllers.

Two XIAO ESP32-S3 microcontrollers sit on the platform — one drives eight relays that fire the solenoids; the other monitors auxiliary sensors. An ESP-NOW link carries 8-bit thruster commands from a ground-station gateway. Pose comes from seven OptiTrack Prime 13 cameras at 240 Hz around a 6 × 6 m epoxy floor. Mixed-integer MPC is solved on the ground station at 16.7 Hz; only on/off commands go to the vehicle.

Design iterations

January 2025 concept versus the final build

A January 2025 design proposal captured an earlier concept. Before cutting hardware I also evaluated triangular, square, and hexagonal bearing layouts, alternative thruster placements, and different air-supply configurations in CAD. The square, eight-thruster layout was selected for redundancy without making the discrete control problem too large to solve in 60 ms. After assembly I adjusted the bearings along the machined grooves until support loads were within ±1.8% of equal, to reduce steady-state torque bias.

Testing & results

Hardware on the epoxy floor, compared with the digital twin

Closed-loop tests were run on the 6 × 6 m epoxy floor under OptiTrack. In the profile-following hardware test, mean tracking error was 0.179 m, with a final position error of 0.024 m and a final heading error of 0.51°. The mixed-integer MPC ran at 16.7 Hz (60 ms period). Mean solve time was 4.61 ms, and every cycle met the 60 ms deadline. Total thruster-on time agreed with the simulation to within 1.5%.
